I. Project Overview

In a modern media landscape marked by push notifications, short-form media, and inattentive social media scrolling tendencies, the headline has become the entirety of the story for many readers and research shows that news consumers are increasingly failing to click through to read full articles. Consequently, this implies that how a headline is worded—and thus the emotions it evokes and the political valence it carries—is some of the only information that increasingly more consumers are gleaning from traditional print media. This makes the headline an increasingly important point of investigation for understanding modern media's implications in American politics, to determine if headlines have truly become more politically polarized and emotionally charged over time. Yet, headlines remain a largely unexplored domain in both political science and natural language processing.

Thus, our project primarily tries to answer, is there a transformer-based classifier model that, after being trained on short, noisy headline data, can detect ideological bias? Second, motivated by the hypothesis that partisan outlets may increasingly use emotionally-laden language, does incorporating emotionality as a secondary training signal in a dual-headed model improve ideology classification? Lastly, if the model can accomplish this, does inference over a decade of other headlines reveal time-varying trends in either of these dimensions? While addressing a major gap in political science research, these questions also address key questions about applications and capabilities of multi-task learning and machine learning model applications in political science.

II. Literature Review and Our Contributions

A. The Importance of Machine Learning in Headline and Media Bias Research

Political scientists have long sought out explanations of media bias. For instance, popular research focuses on how media ideological bias is economically motivated by outlets having commercial incentives to confirm and conform to the ideologies of their largely ideologically homogenous audiences (Gentzkow & Shapiro, 2010; DellaVigna & Kaplan, 2007). However, in recent years, changes in the media landscape are contributing to a renewed political science interest in media bias as social media platforms and algorithmic recommendations amplify ideologically extreme content because it maximizes engagement.

With new technologies transforming the political media environment, the application of NLP and machine learning becomes crucial. Researchers have long faced the challenge of scalability: hand-coding thousands of article headlines for political ideology is both expensive and time-consuming. NLP and machine learning methods thus offer tools for enabling headline research. However, applying these methods to headline text presents distinct challenges. Headlines, being shorter, briefer, and more ambiguous than full articles, tend to struggle with methods developed for longer-form text. Thus, our project attempts to address this critical gap in existing scholarship to assess future opportunities for machine learning applications.

B. NLP Approaches to Ideology Detection

Transformers—beginning with BERT in 2018 and expanding to RoBERTa, DistilBERT, and GPT-family models—have dramatically improved performance on ideology classification tasks, and have come to be the primary automated media bias detection mechanisms in political science, according to Rodrigo-Ginés et al. (2024). Achieving significantly higher accuracy and pinpointing fine-grain bias by learning contextual embeddings that capture subtle framing differences, these models can even distinguish tone differences between "undocumented immigrant" and "illegal alien," while frequency-based methods cannot.

In Spinde et al. (2021), researchers introduced the BABE dataset (Bias Annotations By Experts) and used multi-task transformer fine-tuning to detect media bias in full news articles. They found that multi-task learning—jointly predicting bias type, intensity, and presence—outperformed single-task classifiers, a finding that directly informs our decision to jointly model ideology and emotional sensationalism with the goal of enriching ideological modeling. Recent work comparing transformer families on bias detection (Banik et. al., 2025) further found that RoBERTa achieves the highest alignment with human labels among traditional transformer architectures.

These findings thus directly impact our core architectural decision: we train a single, dual-headed DistilBERT backbone to test whether joint supervision over ideology and emotionality improves performance in political ideology bias, while using RoBERTa as a stronger single-task baseline to isolate whether any performance gains are attributable to multi-task learning specifically or simply to backbone quality.

C. The Distinctive Challenge of Headlines versus Full Articles

Most existing bias-detection literature operates on full article bodies, not headlines. This matters for two reasons. First, headlines are far shorter, which means there is less textual signal available to the classifier. Additionally, the scarcity of headline-specific labeled datasets for emotionality classification is a key motivation for our use of active learning. Rather than relying solely on existing article-level ideology labels, we augment existing article-level labels with crowdsourced headline-level annotations from our classmates, to generate a training signal not previously explored in existing literature.

D. Emotional Valence and Ideology as a Joint Task

In light of the recent literature, this report hypothesizes that ideology (political alignment) is meaningfully related to emotional valence (emotional intensity) in headlines. However, existing literature primarily studies ideology and emotionality separately and has relied on different linguistic signals and annotation schemes across research.

For instance, some emotionality research uses the SENS-HEAD dataset which labels headlines for emotional intensity, but also linguistic features like punctuation and syntax to indicate emotional cues; but, even with this labeling, research using this data finds only moderate performance (F1 = .66) (Chang et. al., 2025). On the other hand, transformer models that rely more on contextual embeddings to learn bias like RoBERTa-Large have performed well on these sort of classification tasks (Alarfaj et. al., 2025).

These examples show how the most successful emotionality classification methods have been achieved using similar, transformer-based models as ideology modeling, suggesting potential for successful joint modeling of the two dimensions. This project targets this opportunity by modeling both jointly, testing whether a shared representation can better capture ideology via emotionality in the limited and highly constructed signals present in headline text.

E. Temporal Analysis as a Downstream Application

Time-varying analysis of media bias is rare in the computational literature overall, but some time-varying research has focused on paragraph-level annotations to track ideology around specific policies (Rodrigo-Ginés et al. 2024). Recent research by Lyu et al. (2024) tracked changes in headline ideology from 2014 to 2022, but notably did not control for topic-mix. Thus, we find a gap in the literature surrounding time-varying headline research—especially research that controls for topic-mix. Topic-mix is a notable potential confounder—outlets might have begun to cover more polarizing topics in later years, making headlines appear more extreme without intentional editorial changes.

Thus, after building our model, we apply it to the QBIAS dataset to generate ideology and emotionality scores for articles across four major U.S. news outlets between 2013 to 2022. Here, our time-series design explicitly addresses the above confounds by using the topic tags in the QBIAS dataset to run topic-stratified trend analyses—comparing ideology scores for, say, economic coverage in 2013 vs. 2022 holding topic constant. This is the core methodological contribution that distinguishes our work from prior efforts.

III. Methodology and Model Architecture

A. Data

Training labels — Ideology: We use the QBIAS Media Bias in Search Queries dataset of 21,754 headlines labeled left (−1), center (0), or right (1) by political ideology, and which also includes topic tags enabling topic-stratified trend analyses.

Training labels — Emotionality: We generate these via a pairwise comparison app deployed to classmates. These pairwise preferences are converted into continuous emotionality scores using win rate, producing 1,095 labeled headlines that serve as the emotionality signal for the dual-head model.

Inference Data: We use the All The News 2.0 dataset sourced from Kaggle. This dataset contains 4.5M headlines across the 10 largest U.S. news sites from 2007–2022, which we filter to include CNN, Fox News, The Washington Post, and The New York Times in the 2013–2022 window, yielding 1,068,680 headlines for downstream time-series analysis.

B. Active Learning Design

A scale-based question in the format "on a scale from 1–5" is likely to introduce rating-scale bias. To counteract this, we instead operationalize emotionality by presenting the user with a pair of headlines and ask which of the two headlines "uses more emotional, alarming, or value-laden language?" This generates a ranking signal that is more reliable and consistent across annotators.

We then compute emotionality scores as win rates from pairwise comparisons. While the Bradley-Terry model was initially implemented to convert preferences into continuous scores, the sparse comparison structure of our annotation set—most headlines receiving few comparisons—means win rate provides equivalent information with better numerical properties for downstream binary classification. The active learning live annotation interface can be found linked in our GitHub repository, or alternatively, via this URL: https://headline-shift.streamlit.app/.

C. Model Architecture

Our architecture is motivated by a question based on gaps in the multi-task learning literature: do ideology and emotionality share enough representational structure in headline text that joint training produces better ideological understandings than training ideology alone? If partisan outlets systematically use more emotionally charged language as we hypothesize, then a shared encoder trained on both signals should learn richer representations than one trained on a single task. Notably, here we treat emotionality as a secondary training signal rather than a target task. Our goal is not to build a standalone emotionality classifier independent of ideology, but to test whether emotionality supervision regularizes the shared backbone in ways that improve ideology detection.

Our primary model is a fine-tuned DistilBERT-base (uncased) backbone with two task-specific heads attached to the [CLS] token. DistilBERT retains ~97% of BERT's performance at 40% fewer parameters — important given inference over 1,068,680 headlines. The ideology head projects from 768 to 256 dimensions with GELU activation and 3-class softmax. The emotionality head is shallower (768 to 128, sigmoid output), reflecting that binary emotionality requires less transformation of the shared representation. The joint objective is:

L = 2.0 × CrossEntropy(ŷ₁, y₁) + 0.5 × BCE(ŷ₂, y₂)

Loss weights and learning rate (3e-5) were selected via a 9-configuration validation set search over three learning rates and three loss weight schemes before any test set evaluation. Since ideology labels cover all 21,754 headlines while emotionality labels cover only our 1,095 annotated headlines, we cycle the smaller dataset during training and backpropagate the summed loss through the shared backbone each step. We train four models in total, designed as a 2×2 experiment across backbone and training paradigm, and the model architectures are further visualized in Figure 2:

  • Model 1 — TF-IDF + Logistic Regression (Ideology Only): Our non-neural baseline, establishing the performance floor for bag-of-words methods.
  • Model 2: DistilBERT Ideology-Only Ablation: Identical to our primary model without the emotionality head. Any F1 difference is attributable solely to the joint emotionality supervision signal.
  • Model 3: DistilBERT Dual-Head: Jointly trains ideology and emotionality heads through a shared backbone, directly testing our multi-task hypothesis.
  • Model 4: RoBERTa Ideology-Only: Since RoBERTa achieves the highest human-label alignment among traditional transformer architectures (Banik et. al., 2025), running this single-task model isolates backbone quality from training paradigm, so any remaining performance difference between this and our primary model is attributable specifically to multi-task learning.
  • Model 5: RoBERTa Dual-Head Extended Comparison: Same dual-head architecture applied to RoBERTa, completing the 2×2 design and testing whether the multi-task result holds across a stronger backbone.

E. Evaluation

For ideology classification, our primary metric is macro-averaged F1, which averages F1 equally across all three classes—essential given our class imbalance (a classifier always predicting "left" achieves 47% accuracy but near-zero macro F1). We supplement this with Cohen's Kappa, which measures agreement corrected for chance, indicating whether the model is genuinely learning or exploiting class priors.

Crucially, we do not report a standalone emotionality F1, for both methodological and data limitation reasons. Methodologically, because emotionality labels are used exclusively as a training signal during fine-tuning, evaluating the emotionality head on those same headlines would measure memorization rather than generalization. On the data side, the small overlap between our 1,095 emotionality-labeled headlines and the ideology test split produces an inadequate single-class subset, making binary F1 undefined or trivially perfect. A proper emotionality evaluation would require a held-out annotated test set that was never seen during training — a resource we did not have access to given the constraints of crowdsourced annotation at this scale. For similar reasons, AUC-ROC is not reported, as our annotation structure (most headlines compared exactly once) would produce a sparse score distribution that would result in undefined AUC on the test split. The contribution of emotionality supervision is therefore assessed indirectly: any improvement in ideology F1 between dual-head and single-task models is attributable to joint training.

For the time-series downstream application, we report mean ideology and emotionality scores per outlet per year with 95% confidence intervals, supplemented by topic-stratified trends as a robustness check. We look for if trends disappear after conditioning on the topic, as this may be evidence that the coverage-mix has shifted, as opposed to seeing true editorial shifts in ideology.

F. Hypothesis and Expected Results

Our primary hypothesis is that training ideology and emotionality jointly will produce richer representations than training ideology alone, improving ideology F1 over both the TF-IDF baseline and a single-task DistilBERT ablation — consistent with the multi-task findings of Spinde et al. (2021). Furthermore, we hypothesize that any gains from the dual-head model reflect the multi-task training signal specifically rather than backbone quality alone — tested by comparing against single-task RoBERTa, which isolates architectural strength from training paradigm. If dual-head DistilBERT outperforms RoBERTa single-task, the improvement is attributable to joint training rather than architecture. Applied to the dated Kaggle corpus, we further hypothesize that the resulting scores will reveal measurable outlet-level temporal drift in both ideology and emotionality between 2013 and 2022.

Secondary hypotheses on the time-series outputs include the following: (1) emotionality scores increase more sharply in the 2016–2020 period coinciding with the Trump presidency; (2) ideology drift is larger on political and social topics than economic or international coverage.

We remain open to negative results. If the multi-task model does not outperform single-task baselines, that finding is itself informative about whether ideology and emotionality share useful representational structure in short headline text. If time-series trends disappear after topic-stratification, that is a meaningful null result about the nature of apparent media drift.

Our five final tuned models are summarized above. All of the transformer models outperform the TD-IDF baselines (F1 = 0.11), confirming our earlier hypothesis: transformer models capture more ideological framing than bag-of-words methods. Yet, despite performing better than TF-IDF, these F1-scores are noticeably low. Meanwhile, the RoBERTa single-task shows the strongest performance (F1 = 0.47, Kappa = 0.21), followed by RoBERTa dual-head (F1 = 0.45), DistilBERT ablation (F1 = 0.43), and DistilBERT dual-head (F1 = 0.42). Thus, our results suggest that while transformer models can perform better at ideological bias detection than basic bag-of-words methods, ideological bias remains difficult to capture in headlines, even using more advanced architectures.

The training curves reveal that training loss decreases steadily across all five epochs and validation F1 peaks at epoch 4 before dropping slightly at epoch 5, suggesting mild overfitting expected when fine-tuning a 66M+ parameter model on 15,000 examples. All reported test results use the best validation checkpoint to mitigate this.

Overall, these results and the 2×2 design help reveal two findings. First, backbone quality is the dominant factor: RoBERTa outperforms DistilBERT in both single-task and dual-head conditions, consistent with RoBERTa's more aggressive pre-training and stronger general language representations. Second, and more importantly for our hypothesis, multi-task training consistently hurts ideology F1 regardless of backbone—DistilBERT single-task outperforms DistilBERT dual-head (0.43 vs. 0.42), and RoBERTa single-task outperforms RoBERTa dual-head (0.47 vs. 0.45). This constitutes a robust negative result on our core hypothesis: contrary to the findings of Spinde et al. (2021), joint emotionality supervision does not improve ideology classification in our research.

We attribute this to likely being due to annotation sparsity—our 1,095 headlines were each compared only once or twice, producing a coarse binary signal that introduces noise into the shared backbone rather than providing useful regularization. Spinde et al. worked with expert-annotated datasets with substantially richer coverage, suggesting that the multi-task benefit is dependent on annotation density and may emerge with larger emotionality datasets, which unfortunately we did not have access to.

A Cohen's Kappa of .21 on the best-performing model—the RoBERTa single-task—suggests that while there is genuine ideological signal being learned beyond what random guessing may produce, substantial uncertainty remains. As shown in Figure 6, center headlines are the hardest classification category across all models, with F1 scores ranging from 0.10 (TF-IDF) to 0.33 (RoBERTa single-task) — far below performance on left and right headlines. This is consistent with neutral framing lacking the distinctive vocabulary that partisan headlines carry: center headlines are defined by their ideological ambiguity, making them genuinely difficult to classify even for humans, let alone a model trained on 7–15 word inputs.

B. Limitations and Discussion

Our core hypothesis that joint emotionality supervision would improve ideology classification was not supported. We ran six variations of the dual-head model across different annotation sizes, epochs, learning rates, and loss weight schemes, and the result was consistent: the emotionality signal introduces noise rather than useful regularization. The most likely explanation is annotation sparsity: with each of our 1,095 headlines compared exactly once, the emotionality signal is too coarse (binary win/loss) and too small relative to 15,000 ideology examples to regularize the shared backbone effectively. Spinde et al. (2021), whose multi-task finding motivated our design, worked with substantially richer annotation coverage—a key difference.

A secondary limitation is the modest absolute performance of all models. The best-performing model having a Macro F1 of 0.47 on a three-class problem reflects the genuine difficulty of ideology classification from 7–15 word headlines, where textual signal is sparse and inter-annotator agreement on ideology labels is imperfect, even for humans. Future work with larger annotated datasets and longer training runs may improve these numbers, but the relative ordering of our models is likely to be stable.

V. Downstream Application: Time-Series Analysis

We run inference using the best-performing model (RoBERTa single-task, Test Macro F1 = 0.47) on all 1,068,680 dated headlines from CNN, Fox News, Washington Post, and New York Times from 2013–2022. While this F1 reflects the genuine difficulty of ideology classification from short headline text, the model meaningfully exceeds both the random baseline and the TF-IDF floor, and produces directionally interpretable ideology scores that are consistent with known outlet-level political leanings—making the downstream time-series patterns worth examining, with appropriate caution and note that we do not assess emotionality.

A. Ideology Trends

Figure 7 reflects the quarterly mean ideology scores across the outlets of interest from 2013 to 2022, with 95% confidence intervals shaded. Overall, the patterns are not that reflective of increasing polarization across the decade. The most standout pattern is Fox News's steady rightward shift from 2018 onward—coinciding with the Trump presidency—after beginning near zero at the start of the period. Fox News crosses into a right-classified ideology around 2019-2020 and reaches approximately +0.25 by late 2022. Meanwhile, CNN, The Washington Post, and the NYT remain left-classified throughout the period—even deepening slightly in the latter half—with Washington Post remaining the most stably left. The NYT shows an unexpected shift toward -0.25 in 2014-2015 before reverting, while CNN appears to be the most volatile, spiking towards zero in mid-2017 and then dropping back in 2019.

Figure 8 shows a similar story, with Fox News's left-classified proportion shrinking steadily through the period (from 45-50% to 33%) and its right-classified proportions increasing in tandem. The Washington Post's left proportions remain stable, while the NYT noticeably increases in its left share from 2013 to 2019. CNN's 2018 scores are the most notable discontinuity in the data, likely reflecting a shift in topic coverage effects rather than a real, intentional editorial shift.

B. Topic-Stratified Analysis

Figure 9 shows that Fox News is the only outlet to fall into a positive (right-leaning) classification. Notably, the most polarized scores appear for topics with a relevant partisan salience, such as immigration (+0.29), general politics (+0.21), and climate (+0.16). The outlet is far less polarized in relation to topics such as crime (-0.01), education (+0.03), and technology (+0.03). CNN, Washington Post, and the NYT are negative across all topics, with the Washington Post remaining the most stably left-leaning outlet. The patterns shown here evidence that our model is capturing real framing differences across outlets, not just noise, and are consistent with the hypothesis that this divergence is largest on the most politically-charged topics.

C. Emotionality Effects

Since emotionality was incorporated as a secondary training signal rather than a target task, we conducted the downstream inference using the best performing model—the RoBERTa single-task—which produces ideology scores only. Thus, we do not conduct downstream inference on emotionality scores.

VI. Conclusion

A. Summary

This project set out to answer whether transformer-based multi-task learning could improve ideology classification in news headlines by jointly modeling emotionality as a secondary training signal, and whether the resulting classification applications reveal ideological shifts in major American news outlets. To the first point, our findings are resoundingly negative: joint emotionality supervision consistently hurts ideology F1, even with stronger backbones, greater annotation volume, and improved hyperparameter configurations. In fact, backbone quality—not multi-task learning—proved to be the more important factor in model performance; the RoBERTa single-task model emerged as the best model (F1 = 0.47, Kappa = 0.21). Further, downstream inference reveals an important outlet divergence: Fox News trends toward ideological balance over the 2013–2022 period while CNN, Washington Post, and New York Times remain stable and left-classified, with the divergence most pronounced on politically salient topics like immigration and healthcare.

B. Future Considerations

The most important limitation of this work is annotation sparsity. Our 1,095 emotionality labels produced a signal too coarse to regularize the shared backbone. If given more time, we would have carried out more thorough annotation campaigns with classmates. As such, future work should collect denser pairwise annotations (ideally 5–10 comparisons per headline) and re-test the multi-task hypothesis, which may well hold at higher annotation density as Spinde et al.'s results with full-length articles suggest. Beyond improving annotation data quality, future work could also explore running RoBERTa dual-head with a substantially larger emotionality dataset to isolate whether the negative multi-task result is specific to DistilBERT's capacity or a more general property of the task.

C. Ethical Considerations

Because these classification systems rely on relatively subjective understandings of what constitutes "left", "right", or "center", any system that automatically classifies content ideologies carries inherent risks. The training labels used here come from AllSides, a single source with its own methodological assumptions and biases. Models trained on these labels thus necessarily carry the ideological framework that AllSides uses, which notably has the potential to not generalize across cultures, time periods, geographies, or definitions of political center.

Beyond this, tools that quantify media bias can be weaponized to delegitimize journalism or confirm pre-existing political priors rather than inform neutral analysis. We caution that our model's outputs should be interpreted as relative signals within a specific training distribution, not objective ground truth about outlet ideology, and that any deployment of such a system in public-facing contexts warrants careful consideration of how bias classifications are framed and communicated.
